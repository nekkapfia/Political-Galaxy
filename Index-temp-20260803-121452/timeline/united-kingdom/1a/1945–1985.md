## **1945–1985: Post-War Common-Law Baseline – Base Score: 78**

The four decades after 1945 preserved a relatively high-autonomy environment for personal expression and association in the United Kingdom. The dominant legal and political self-understanding remained rooted in the common-law tradition of residual liberty: the subject was free to speak, publish, assemble and associate unless a specific, narrowly construed criminal prohibition applied. Statutory content-based speech offences were limited. Public-order powers and older residual offences (blasphemy, obscenity, certain sedition-related provisions) existed, but they had not yet been transformed into the broad modern toolkit that would appear with the Public Order Act 1986. The practical environment for voluntary political speech, criticism of government, and public debate therefore remained comparatively open by later British standards and by contemporary European standards.

### Core 1A Relevant Restrictions / Elements – Expanded Analysis

* Core criminal prohibitions on homicide, rape, assault, true threats, and direct physical harm. These form the protective baseline and sit in the high-autonomy bands (95–100 / 90–95 range for pure direct-harm protection).
* Limited residual public-order powers and older common-law or statutory offences of blasphemy, obscenity and certain forms of seditious libel. Enforcement intensity remained relatively narrow and episodic by later standards.
* Absence of the modern Section 5 “harassment, alarm or distress” standard that would be introduced in 1986.
* Absence of the Part III racial-hatred / stirring-up offences in their contemporary statutory form.
* Communications and postal offences existed in narrower pre-2003 form but lacked the breadth, digital reach and enforcement intensity of later layers.
* No comprehensive statutory framework equivalent to the later Online Safety Act or the dense post-1986 public-order speech regime.
* Association and assembly remained governed primarily by ordinary public-order and conspiracy rules rather than by expansive content-based or identity-based restrictions.
* Ordinary protective criminal law continued to operate as the primary constraint on interpersonal violence; political and expressive restrictions remained secondary.

The restrictive architecture of the era is therefore dominated by a strong protective baseline against direct harm, overlaid with only modest statutory constraints on expression. The common-law presumption in favour of liberty still structured the practical environment for voluntary speech, publication and association to a greater degree than in any subsequent British era. Political criticism of government, robust public debate, and the circulation of controversial ideas operated under relatively light statutory constraint. The period sits clearly above the denser speech regimes that would follow, while remaining below a pure theoretical minimal-restriction score because ordinary protective criminal law and residual public-order powers continued to exist.

**Key Shift:** Continuity of the post-1945 legal order with minimal new content-based speech legislation, preserving a relatively high-autonomy baseline grounded in common-law residual liberty.

### Why this produces a final score of 78

Negative-Only Weighting applied to the strong protective baseline and the limited contemporaneous statutory speech controls yields a Base Score of 78. The score reflects a system that had not yet enacted the major expansions of non-violent speech offences that would begin in 1986. Inheritance from equivalent high-autonomy protective baselines supports this placement. No Endos/Xenos split is warranted: no stable, system-wide, discretely targetable out-group differential of the required intensity existed. The placement is consistent with a jurisdiction still operating primarily under the older common-law speech environment rather than under the later statutory densification.
