# Core 1A: United Kingdom – 1986–2000
## Public Order Act and Early Hate-Speech Expansion
**Base Score: 68**  
**Xenos Score: N/A** (no split)

### Overview

The Public Order Act 1986 marks the first major statutory densification of speech controls in the post-war period and the decisive downward break from the high common-law baseline of 1945–1985. Section 5 and the Part III racial-hatred provisions introduced broader, audience-focused standards for criminalising expression. These changes were durable; they were not emergency measures and they permanently altered the restrictive architecture. Enforcement practice through the 1990s normalised the use of the new instruments against a range of public and political speech. The result is a clear and lasting reduction in the practical autonomy of voluntary expression relative to the preceding era.

### Core 1A Relevant Restrictions / Elements

* Public Order Act 1986 Section 5: criminalisation of the use of threatening, abusive or insulting words or behaviour, or disorderly behaviour, within the hearing or sight of a person likely to be caused harassment, alarm or distress. This is a direct content-based restriction on voluntary expression and sits well below the high-autonomy bands.
* Part III of the 1986 Act: offences of stirring up racial hatred through words, written material, public performances and recordings. These expanded the scope of prohibited political and public speech beyond the older, narrower residual offences.
* Subsequent enforcement practice in the late 1980s and 1990s that applied the new provisions to demonstrations, publications, speeches and everyday public interactions.
* Retention of the core direct-harm criminal prohibitions (violence, true threats, assault).
* Continued absence of the later Communications Act 2003 Section 127 breadth and of any Online Safety Act-style platform duties.
* Residual older offences (blasphemy, obscenity) remained but were secondary to the new statutory layer.

### Expanded Analysis

The era is defined by the replacement of a relatively narrow common-law and residual statutory environment with a more expansive legislative framework for non-violent speech offences. Section 5 shifted the legal threshold from relatively clear categories of direct threat or disorder toward a broader standard keyed to the likely emotional impact on an audience. Part III simultaneously created a specific content-based regime around racial hatred. Together these instruments constituted the first post-war statutory architecture that systematically lowered the cost of ordinary political and social speech. Enforcement was not uniform, yet the existence and regular use of the new powers is what is scored under Direct Restriction Only. Negative-Only Weighting records the addition of these layers on top of the prior baseline; no positive credit is available for the retention of residual liberties that had already been scored in the previous era.

No system-wide Endos/Xenos split is applied. While certain communities experienced higher practical intensity of enforcement, the statutory framework itself was general rather than a formal dual legal system of the type that triggers the optional split.

**Key Shift:** Introduction of the modern statutory architecture for non-violent speech offences that cause alarm or distress or stir up racial hatred, permanently lowering the autonomy baseline from the 1945–1985 level.

### Why this produces a Base Score of 68

Addition of the Section 5 and racial-hatred layers to the prior high baseline of 78 produces a clear downward movement of ten points under Negative-Only Weighting. The restrictions are direct prohibitions on voluntary expression rather than mere punishments, processes or chilling effects. The score is consistent with the cumulative weight of the new statutory instruments and with inheritance from equivalent public-order speech restrictions scored elsewhere in the Core 1A corpus. The placement remains above the later post-2001 and post-2023 scores because the denser Communications Act and Online Safety Act layers had not yet been added.
