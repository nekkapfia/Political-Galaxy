# Core 1A: United Kingdom – Green Party of England and Wales
## Personal Autonomy Analysis
**Endos Score: 48**  
**Xenos Score: 42**

### Overview

The Green Party frames personal autonomy within an ecological and social-justice matrix that prioritises protection from harm, including online and identity-based harm. It supports robust regulation of online platforms, expansive interpretations of hate speech, and institutional measures against discrimination that frequently translate into content and expression controls. The party’s platform does not propose any narrowing of the existing Public Order Act, Communications Act or Online Safety Act architecture; instead it favours stronger enforcement and broader application of identity-based restrictions. The result is one of the more restrictive profiles among major British parties.

### Core 1A Relevant Policies / Elements

* Support for the Online Safety Act’s duties on platforms to remove illegal content and protect users from harmful material, with explicit emphasis on identity-based and ecological harms.
* Advocacy for stronger enforcement of stirring-up-hatred offences and related Public Order Act provisions, particularly where speech is characterised as racist, transphobic, or otherwise identity-hostile.
* Institutional and educational measures that treat certain forms of critical speech (on gender, race, or immigration) as potentially harmful and therefore subject to restriction or institutional sanction.
* Support for platform-level content moderation and algorithmic interventions aimed at reducing the visibility of speech deemed discriminatory or harmful.
* Retention and potential expansion of Equality Act-related speech and association constraints in public and quasi-public settings.
* Opposition to repeal of existing hate-speech or online-safety frameworks.
* No proposal to narrow Section 5 of the Public Order Act or the communications offences that currently criminalise a wide range of non-violent expression.

### Expanded Analysis

The party’s restrictive profile is driven by the combination of retained baseline offences and an operative preference for broader application of identity-based speech controls and platform duties. While formal doctrine emphasises justice and ecological limits, the practical effect under Core 1A rules is a denser set of direct restrictions on voluntary expression and association than parties further up the autonomy scale. Support for expansive enforcement of stirring-up and related provisions, together with institutional measures that treat critical speech on protected characteristics as potentially harmful, increases the cumulative weight of content-based controls. The absence of any proposal to narrow the Public Order Act, Communications Act or Online Safety Act architecture, coupled with affirmative support for stronger application, produces a lower score.

A modest Endos/Xenos differential is recorded. The party’s platform and institutional practice apply heightened scrutiny and potential restriction to speech critical of certain protected identity categories (particularly around race, gender identity and related topics). This creates a stable, discretely targetable divergence in the intensity of expression controls, satisfying the optional-split rule. The differential is not as wide as formal dual legal systems, but it is consistent and observable.

**Key Shift relative to current baseline:** Affirmative support for intensified identity-based enforcement and platform moderation on top of the already dense post-2023 architecture, producing a downward movement from the status-quo score and a limited but justified Endos/Xenos split.

### Why this produces Endos 48 / Xenos 42

The cumulative weight of support for the Online Safety Act, expansive hate-speech enforcement, and identity-based institutional controls yields an Endos score of 48 under Negative-Only Weighting. A modest Xenos differential (42) is recorded because the party’s platform and institutional practice apply heightened scrutiny and potential restriction to speech critical of certain protected identity categories, creating a stable, discretely targetable outgroup differential in the intensity of expression controls. No credit is given for rights language or ecological framing. The scores sit at the more restrictive end of the contemporary British party spectrum, consistent with a preference for broader rather than narrower application of content-based speech controls.
