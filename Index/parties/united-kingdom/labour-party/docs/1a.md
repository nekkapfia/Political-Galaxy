# Core 1A: United Kingdom – Labour Party
## Personal Autonomy Analysis
**Endos Score: 34**  
**Xenos Score: 29**

### Overview

The Labour Party, scored strictly on the restrictions that would exist under its governing programme and weighted toward the recent Starmer government and Andy Burnham’s faction, produces a restrictive Core 1A profile. The final architecture is defined by full retention and active defence of the Online Safety Act, the addition of election-period and political-content platform duties, the institutionalisation of an official anti-Muslim hostility definition, the complete retention of Public Order Act, Racial and Religious Hatred Act and communications offences, and further under-16 social-media and device-level measures. Framing, child-protection language and accuracy justifications are discarded under Direct Restriction Only and Negative-Only Weighting. The cumulative weight of the final set places the party below pure baseline-retention parties and above the denser Green/SNP identity-orthodoxy cluster, with a limited Endos/Xenos differential driven by the anti-Muslim hostility institutional layer.

Faction weighting is explicit. Primary weight is given to the Starmer government’s observable practice since 2024 and to Andy Burnham’s soft-left positioning. Burnham does not offer a liberalising counter-weight on speech; available signals point toward continuity or mild intensification of platform and press controls. Residual Corbynite positions are recorded but treated as non-decisive for the final score.

### Core 1A Relevant Policies / Elements

* Full retention, defence and active implementation of the Online Safety Act 2023, including illegal-content duties, age-assurance requirements, platform accountability for harmful material, and the regulatory apparatus under Ofcom. Neither the Starmer government nor the Burnham soft-left faction advances a repeal or substantial narrowing agenda; the Act is treated as settled architecture to be enforced and, where necessary, tightened.
* Election-period and political-content platform duties, including measures that impose obligations on platforms regarding the amplification, visibility and sharing of political information during election periods, in ways analogous to broadcast impartiality rules. These extend content control into the practical reach of political speech online. Burnham-linked commentary has included openness to stronger regulation of press and platform power.
* Adoption and institutional use of an official definition of “anti-Muslim hostility” intended to guide public-sector, policing and potentially legal responses. The definition functions as an institutional lens that raises the practical risk of restriction, investigation or sanction for speech critical of Islam, Islamic doctrine or Islamist politics relative to other forms of religious or ideological criticism.
* Maintenance in full of the Public Order Act 1986 (including Section 5 “harassment, alarm or distress” and stirring-up provisions), the Racial and Religious Hatred Act, the Communications Act 2003 and the Malicious Communications Act. There is no programme to narrow these offences or to raise the threshold for non-violent expression.
* Further child-protection and access-restriction measures, including under-16 social-media restrictions and support for device-level scanning or access controls. These are direct constraints on communication and information access for a defined age cohort and form part of the retained regulatory surface.
* Continued operation of institutional equality, diversity and hate-speech frameworks across the public sector. Speech critical of protected characteristics remains subject to hostile-environment framing, potential non-crime hate-incident recording, and disciplinary risk in employment and institutional settings.
* Absence of mandatory attitude-correction training as a central, party-defining national programme of the type evidenced under Green-influenced councils. EDI frameworks remain embedded, but the compulsory belief-correction layer is not the decisive national feature it is for the Green Party.

### Expanded Analysis

Under pure final-set scoring only the restrictions listed above are counted. The Starmer government has not merely inherited the post-2023 architecture; it has defended it, operationalised it, and added concrete expansions. The Online Safety Act is treated as a permanent regulatory floor. Election-period platform duties extend the same logic into the visibility of political speech at the moment it matters most for democratic competition. The anti-Muslim hostility definition introduces a targeted institutional vulnerability for a discrete category of religious and political criticism. Under-16 and device-level measures add further direct constraints on access and communication. Framing is ignored.

Andy Burnham’s faction is not a liberalising force on these questions. Soft-left positioning on economics and public services does not translate into a speech-liberalisation agenda. Signals around press regulation and platform power point toward continuity or mild intensification rather than rollback. Residual Corbynite currents within the wider party often favour denser controls on speech related to Israel, “anti-imperialism” and related identity categories, but these are not treated as the decisive governing programme for this score.

Each retained element is a direct prohibition or regulatory compulsion. The anti-Muslim hostility definition is particularly significant for the Xenos column: it creates a stable, institutionally reinforced intensity differential. Speech critical of Islam or Islamism faces a heightened risk of institutional scrutiny relative to parallel criticism of other religions or ideologies. This satisfies the optional-split rule at a limited but real level.

The architecture is heavier than pure baseline retention (Liberal Democrats, Conservatives) because of the concrete expansions (anti-Muslim hostility definition, election-period duties, under-16 measures). It is lighter than the Green package because mandatory attitude-correction training is not a central, party-defining national feature and because the densest identity-orthodoxy layers are less comprehensively operationalised. The cumulative weight therefore sits in the low-to-mid 30s.

**Key Shift:** Active defence and expansion of the Online Safety Act architecture, institutionalisation of an identity-specific hostility definition, election-period platform duties and under-16 access measures as the characteristic governing programme of the recent Labour government and the Burnham-weighted soft left.

### Why this produces Endos 34 / Xenos 29

The cumulative weight of the final restriction set — Online Safety Act retention and enforcement, election-period platform duties, anti-Muslim hostility definition, full public-order and communications architecture, under-16 measures, and residual institutional equality frameworks — places Endos at 34. Heightened institutional risk on speech critical of Islam or Islamism produces Xenos 29. Only the restrictions that remain are scored; framing is ignored. Faction weighting privileges the Starmer government and Burnham soft left; residual Corbynite positions are non-decisive. The placement is consistent with the progressive-party scoring guide: baseline plus concrete identity and platform expansions belongs in the low-to-mid 30s, not in the 50s.
